Project Methodology

How I Collected, Coded, and Compared the Data

This project used a mixed approach that combined social media data collection, post-by-post coding, engagement analysis, ranking context, and qualitative website review. The goal was to compare how universities and business schools present themselves across Instagram, LinkedIn, and official business school websites.

Process Overview

Project workflow

1

Collect posts

I gathered Instagram and LinkedIn posts from general university accounts and business school accounts.

2

Create codebooks

I created codebooks and dropdown menus so posts could be categorized consistently.

3

Analyze themes

I used Google Colab to count themes, calculate percentages, and create charts.

4

Compare engagement

I compared average engagement by account type using likes plus comments.

5

Review websites

I reviewed business school websites for career, ROI, network, culture, academics, practical experience, logistics, and flexibility.

School Sample

Which schools were included?

The project compared ten universities and their business schools. The schools were grouped into three comparison categories: elite academic “unicorns,” massive public counterparts, and direct peer rivals. This grouping helped organize the analysis around different types of institutional identity and market position.

Social Media Collection

How I collected Instagram and LinkedIn data

I scraped Instagram and LinkedIn pages for the basic university accounts and the business school accounts. The data covered approximately the past two years and produced about 200 posts for most programs. This gave me a consistent sample to compare how schools communicate across different platforms and account types.

For Instagram, I compared general university accounts with business school Instagram accounts. For LinkedIn, I reviewed both posts and audience comments. This helped me compare not only what schools posted, but also how audiences responded in a professional platform space.

Coding Process

How I used the codebooks

For each dataset, I created a codebook to make the coding process more consistent. The codebooks included theme categories and short definitions for how each category should be used.

I also added dropdown menus in the spreadsheets so each post could be categorized using the same set of choices instead of typing categories manually each time. This helped reduce spelling differences and made the coded data easier to analyze later in Google Colab.

Each post was coded into a primary theme and, when needed, a secondary theme. The theme categories included career outcomes, prestige and rankings, student life, community and belonging, academic quality, alumni success, employer connections, leadership, experiential learning, admissions, and return on investment.

Theme Analysis

How I analyzed the coded posts

After the posts were coded, I used Google Colab to count how often each theme appeared in the different datasets. I also calculated percentages because the datasets did not all have exactly the same number of rows. Percentages made the platform comparisons more fair than only using raw counts.

I created charts for general Instagram themes, business school Instagram themes, LinkedIn post themes, and LinkedIn comment themes. These charts helped show which messages were most common on each platform and account type.

Engagement Analysis

How I compared account structure and engagement

I also compared engagement for different types of business school Instagram accounts. Some schools used a broader School of Business account, while others used a dedicated MBA or graduate programs account.

To compare these account types, I created a total engagement measure by adding likes and comments together. Then I calculated the average total engagement per post for each account type. This helped me examine whether broader business school accounts or more targeted graduate program accounts received stronger interaction in the sample.

Total engagement was calculated as likes plus comments. This is a simple measure, but it allowed me to compare average interaction across posts.

Ranking Sources

Where the ranking information came from

I included business school ranking information to provide context for the schools in the sample. The rankings were pulled from U.S. News & World Report and Poets&Quants. These rankings were not the main focus of the project, but they helped show how each business school is positioned in the larger MBA market.

I used the rankings as background information on the homepage school profile cards. I also planned to compare ranking context with Instagram engagement as an exploratory analysis to see whether higher-ranked schools automatically received stronger interaction.

Website Review

How I evaluated the business school websites

I reviewed each business school website as a separate part of the project. Unlike Instagram and LinkedIn, the websites were not treated as a feed of posts. Instead, I evaluated them as official marketing spaces.

I looked at each business school website from the perspective of a prospective MBA student. I paid attention to what information was easy to find, what message the school emphasized most, and whether the site made the program feel valuable, welcoming, flexible, and career-focused.

The website review focused on the things I find important in an MBA program: career outcomes, return on investment, network, culture, academic quality, practical experience, logistics, flexibility, and the overall feeling of whether I could imagine being successful there.

Tools Used

What tools supported the project?

The project used spreadsheets for data organization and coding, dropdown menus for consistent theme selection, Google Colab for analysis, and HTML/CSS for the final website. The website was built as a public-facing capstone project to present the data, charts, map, website review, and final findings.

Limitations

What this project does not claim

This project is based on a sample of selected universities and business schools, so the findings are not meant to represent all higher education marketing. The data reflects the accounts and posts included in the sample, not every possible communication channel used by each school.

Engagement was measured using likes and comments, which gives a useful comparison but does not capture every type of audience behavior. Follower counts were not used to calculate engagement rate, so the engagement analysis compares average interaction per post rather than engagement as a percentage of audience size.

The website review also includes personal judgment because it evaluates design, navigation, and the feeling of whether a program appears valuable or supportive. Because of that, the website analysis should be understood as a qualitative comparison rather than a formal ranking system.

Methodology Takeaway

The project combines coded data with digital experience analysis.

The social media analysis helped show what schools posted and how audiences engaged with those posts. The website review added a different layer by looking at how official business school websites present value, opportunity, and student experience. Together, these methods helped compare higher education marketing across platforms, account structures, and digital spaces.
