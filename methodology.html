Research Process

Methodology

This page explains how I collected, organized, coded, and reviewed the data for this project. My goal was to look at how schools present themselves across platforms and how audiences respond to that content.

Methodology overview

For this project, I looked at how selected universities and business schools presented themselves across Instagram, LinkedIn, and their official websites. I treated each platform a little differently because they do not all do the same job. Instagram felt more visual and community-based, LinkedIn felt more professional and response-driven, and websites gave the fuller picture of each program.

I started by coding the posts into themes so I could see what schools were talking about most. After that, I looked more closely at the categories that were not as clear, especially the “other” categories. I did not want “other” to become a throwaway label, so I went back through those posts and comments to see what was actually inside that group.

How I coded the posts

I coded each post based on the main idea I saw in the content. Some posts were easy to place, like posts about rankings, alumni success, student life, school pride, or career outcomes. Others were harder because they had more than one message or did not fit neatly into one of the main themes.

When that happened, I used my best judgment and coded the post by the message that seemed most central. If the post still did not clearly fit, I placed it in an “other” category and reviewed that group again later. This helped me keep the coding more honest instead of forcing every post into a category that did not really fit.

Instagram process

For Instagram, I focused on what schools chose to show visually. I looked at whether the posts were more about student life, school pride, campus culture, career outcomes, rankings, or general brand-building. This helped me see how schools used Instagram to create a feeling around the school.

I also looked more closely at the Instagram posts coded as “other.” Some of those posts were not random, they just did not fit neatly into the main themes. Breaking that group down helped me explain what kind of content was hiding inside the broader label.

LinkedIn process

LinkedIn needed a little more work because I looked at both the posts and the comments. First, I coded the LinkedIn posts by theme. Then I compared those post themes with the average number of comments they received so I could see which types of posts seemed to create more conversation.

After that, I coded the comments themselves. This helped me see not just how much people responded, but what kind of response they gave. Some comments were praise or congratulations, some showed school pride, some asked questions, and some were harder to understand without more context.

I also made a heatmap that connects the top LinkedIn post themes with the comment themes that appeared under those posts. That gave me a better way to look at the relationship between what schools posted and how people responded.

Just like with Instagram, I paid extra attention to the “other” and “other or unclear” categories. For LinkedIn posts, “other” included some broad institutional content that did not fit neatly into one theme. For LinkedIn comments, “other or unclear” included things like short reactions, personal memories, location shoutouts, unclear context, general support, and emoji or tag-only comments.

Website process

For the website part, I looked at the official business school websites instead of social media posts. I paid attention to what a prospective student would probably want to know, like career outcomes, return on investment, networking, culture, academics, practical experience, logistics, flexibility, and overall fit.

One challenge with the website portion was that the schools did not organize their sites in the exact same way. Some information lived on the main business school website, while other schools had more dedicated MBA program pages. Because of that, I focused on the pages that seemed most useful to a prospective MBA student and looked for the same kinds of information across each site, even when the layout or page structure was different.

I also created website awards to show which sites stood out to me in different ways. These were not formal rankings. They were based on what I noticed while reviewing the sites, such as design, navigation, program identity, career and ROI messaging, culture and belonging, and whether the site made success feel clear or attainable.

Notes about the coding process

One important part of this project was realizing that coding is not always perfectly clean. Some posts and comments could have fit more than one theme, so I had to make judgment calls. I tried to stay consistent by focusing on the main message of each post or comment.

The “other” categories were also important because they showed me where the main categories were not enough. Instead of leaving those groups vague, I broke them down again so I could better explain what was actually happening in the data.
