Project Findings

What the Platform Comparison Shows

This project showed me that universities and business schools do not tell the same story everywhere online. Instagram, LinkedIn, and business school websites each seem to do a different job in how schools present themselves.

Main Finding

Schools change their message depending on the platform.

Across the project, Instagram, LinkedIn, and business school websites each had a different purpose. Instagram felt more visual and personal. LinkedIn felt more professional and focused on achievement. Business school websites were where schools gave the full program details and tried to prove the value of the degree.

The simplest way I would explain it is this: Instagram builds the feeling, LinkedIn builds credibility, and websites prove the value.

Instagram

Instagram helped me see the personality side of the schools through campus life, community, student experience, and belonging.

LinkedIn

LinkedIn showed the more professional side, with posts about alumni success, rankings, employer connections, leadership, and career value.

Websites

Websites gave the official program information, including outcomes, academics, rankings, logistics, flexibility, and return on investment.

Finding One

Instagram builds personality, connection, and belonging.

The Instagram data helped me see how schools use visual posts to create a feeling around the school. General university Instagram accounts often showed school spirit, student life, events, and community. Business school Instagram accounts still used visual storytelling, but they were more likely to connect the school to programs, careers, alumni, admissions, and future opportunities.

This mattered because Instagram did not look the same across every account. A general university account usually spoke to a broad school community, while a business school account spoke to a more specific academic and professional audience.

Finding Two

LinkedIn focuses more on credibility and professional success.

LinkedIn worked differently from Instagram. Instead of focusing mostly on school personality or student life, LinkedIn posts were more connected to reputation, rankings, leadership, employers, alumni success, and career outcomes.

The LinkedIn comments were also useful because they showed how people responded in a professional space. A lot of the comments were short and supportive, especially when schools posted about achievements, careers, alumni, or congratulations.

Finding Three

Business school websites are where schools try to prove their value.

The website comparison showed me that business school websites are different from social media. They are not feeds, and they are not just quick posts. They are where schools give the full program story and try to help students decide if the program is worth their time, money, and energy.

The strongest websites made the important details easy to see, including career outcomes, ROI, network, culture, academics, hands-on experience, logistics, flexibility, belonging, and opportunity. The website review helped me see that a strong business school website needs both useful information and a feeling that the student could belong and succeed there.

Finding Four

Main business school accounts had stronger average engagement.

One question I had was whether account structure affected engagement. Some schools used a broader School of Business account, while others used a dedicated MBA or graduate programs account. To compare them, I calculated average total engagement per post by adding likes and comments together.

Account types used in this comparison

I grouped the business school Instagram accounts into two account types for this comparison.

  • School of Business pages: Duke Fuqua, Michigan Ross, Notre Dame Mendoza, and SMU Cox.
  • Dedicated MBA or Graduate Programs pages: Baylor Hankamer Graduate Programs, Northwestern Kellogg School of Management, Vanderbilt Owen Graduate Programs, TCU Neeley Graduate Programs, Georgia Terry Graduate Programs, and UT Austin McCombs MBA.

In this sample, the broader School of Business pages had higher average total engagement per post than the dedicated MBA or Graduate Programs pages. This made sense to me because the main business school pages are usually easier to find, more recognizable, and connected to a larger audience.

The dedicated graduate accounts can speak more directly to MBA or graduate students, but they may also be harder for people to find. A prospective student may search for the main business school first because they may not know the name of a smaller side account. If important graduate program information only lives on that smaller account, it may miss people who start with the main school brand.

Finding Five

Higher rankings did not automatically explain Instagram engagement.

I also compared U.S. News business school rankings with average Instagram engagement to see whether higher-ranked schools automatically got more interaction. The chart gave useful context, but it did not show that ranking alone explains engagement.

Some highly ranked schools, such as Northwestern, Michigan, Duke, and UT Austin, had strong average engagement. But the pattern was not perfect. Notre Dame also had strong engagement even though it was ranked lower than several schools in the sample, while Georgia had lower engagement even though it was not one of the lowest-ranked schools.

This also needs to be read with the account structure finding. Some schools used broader School of Business Instagram accounts, while others used smaller MBA or graduate programs accounts. Those smaller accounts may receive less engagement because they are harder to find, have narrower audiences, or do not have the same built-in visibility as the main business school page.

Overall, this showed me that Instagram engagement is shaped by more than prestige. Ranking may matter, but account visibility, audience size, content style, school identity, and account structure also matter.

Final Takeaway

Digital marketing is not just about what schools post. It is also about where people find it.

Overall, this project showed me that higher education marketing changes depending on the platform, the audience, and the account structure. Instagram helps schools create feeling and identity. LinkedIn helps schools build professional credibility. Websites give the evidence behind the program. The engagement comparisons added another important point: even strong content may perform differently depending on where it is posted and how easy that account is to find.

For business schools, the strongest digital strategy may be one that connects all three layers: visual storytelling on Instagram, professional credibility on LinkedIn, and clear proof of value on the website. It also needs to make important graduate program information easy to find from the main business school brand.
